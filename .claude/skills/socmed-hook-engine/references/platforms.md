# Platforms, Conversion Mode, and Testing

## Visible character limits

A hook that works as a spoken video opener can fail as a caption because the punch lands after the "more" cutoff. Written hooks must land inside the visible window. These limits are approximate and change with devices and app updates, so treat them as targets.

| Where it appears | Visible before cutoff (approx.) | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram feed caption | 125 characters | The punch lands in line one. |
| Instagram Reels caption | 55-80 characters | Shorter still, because the video competes for attention. |
| TikTok caption | 80-100 characters | Same as Reels. On-screen text often matters more. |
| LinkedIn post | 210 characters | More room, but line one still does most of the work. |
| X post | 280 characters total | Often the whole post is the hook. |
| Threads post | ~500 characters total, first line visible in feed | Lead with the charged line. |
| YouTube title | 60 characters | Hard truncation, no fold. |
| Email subject line | 40-50 characters on mobile | Every word earns its place. |
| Landing page headline | 60-80 characters | One breath, easy to scan. |
| Meta ad | Primary text ~125, headline field ~40 | Brutally short. |

**Rules:**
- When the platform is known, write every written hook within its limit. If a hook runs long, rewrite it so the punch lands early. Don't just trim the end.
- When no platform is given, default to the Instagram feed limit (125 characters). A tight hook is easier to expand than a loose one is to compress.
- Before presenting, check every written hook against the limit.

## Written vs. spoken hooks

For video platforms (Reels, TikTok, Shorts, YouTube, Stories), or when the user asks for both, give two versions of each hook:

- **Written:** tight, within the character limit, works as text alone. Used as the caption's first line or as on-screen text.
- **Spoken:** can breathe. Two or three sentences, about three to five seconds of speech (30-50 words). The first sentence still has to hit, and later sentences can build tension that only works with voice.

These aren't the same hook at two lengths. The written version may restructure the idea entirely to front-load the punch.

**On-screen text tip:** the spoken line and the on-screen text can say different things. The spoken line creates tension, and the on-screen text names who it's for. Viewers watching muted still get the hook.

## Conversion mode: headline + subheadline

Use for ads, landing pages, email CTAs, sales pages, and any goal of sign-ups, registrations, clicks, or purchases.

**Headline**
- Under 80 characters.
- One idea. If it needs a comma, it's probably two ideas.
- Most charged word first.
- Works on its own, even if the subheadline never gets read.
- No danglers. If the headline already implies something, don't add a phrase restating it.

**Subheadline**
- Under 100 characters.
- Starts with an action verb, and no two subheadlines in a batch share one. Rotate from: Learn, See, Get, Build, Find, Join, Watch, Turn, Map, Fix, Claim, Plan, Start, Grab, Reclaim, Practice, Create, Save, Book, Try.
- Bridges the headline's tension to the specific offer or event by name ("the free 3-day workshop," "the Thursday masterclass"), never "our program."
- Adds new information. It doesn't restate the headline.

**Example (illustrative):**
- Headline: "Your best clients are already in your inbox."
- Subheadline: "See the 3 follow-up emails that turn old inquiries into booked calls, live on Thursday."

**Don't use conversion mode** for reach or brand goals, for video-only openers, or when the user asks for one-liners.

## Testing hooks

Explain this when the user asks how to know which hook works.

**Two numbers that matter for video:**
- **Hook rate** = 3-second views ÷ impressions. How well the hook stops the scroll.
- **Hold rate** = views to the end (or to a set point) ÷ 3-second views. Whether the content delivers on the hook.

For captions and text posts, use saves, shares, and "see more" clicks where the platform shows them. For emails, open rate tests the subject line.

**Reading the results:**
- High hook rate, high hold rate: a winner. Reuse the pattern.
- High hook rate, low hold rate: the hook works but the content doesn't pay it off. Fix the body.
- Low hook rate, high hold rate: great content that not enough people stop for. Test new hooks on the same content.
- Low on both: drop it and learn from it.

**The method:** write many, pick few. Write about 20 hooks, keep the top 5, test 2 or 3 live (the same content with different hooks is the cleanest test), and build on the winner. Numbers from the user's own audience beat any general rule, including the ones in this skill.
