# Craft reference: direct-response promotion

The method behind every promotion this workflow writes: social plugs, emails, and the landing page brief. Read the part that matches the step you are on. Principles only; no shortcuts around them.

## 1. The two-line diagnosis

Every promotion starts with two decisions, written down before any copy.

**Awareness** is where the reader's mind starts when the promo interrupts them. It decides where each piece opens.

| Stage          | The reader                                    | Open with                                                                            |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Unaware        | Does not feel the problem                     | The reader themselves: a story, an identity claim, a sharp observation               |
| Problem-aware  | Feels the pain, does not know solutions exist | Name the problem more precisely than they can, agitate honestly, reveal the category |
| Solution-aware | Knows the category, not this product          | Outcome plus mechanism                                                               |
| Product-aware  | Knows the product, has not bought             | Differentiation, proof, objections                                                   |
| Most-aware     | Wants it, waiting for a reason                | The offer itself; the copy shrinks to almost all deal                                |

**Sophistication** is how tired the whole market is of this promise. It decides whether the substance of a piece is a claim, a mechanism, proof, or identity.

| Level | Market state         | What works                                                                      |
| ----- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | New market           | A simple direct claim                                                           |
| 2     | Claim inflation      | Bigger, faster claims, which burn trust quickly                                 |
| 3     | Mechanism            | A named, specific reason the promise comes true                                 |
| 4     | Mechanism escalation | Easier and faster versions of the how; proof and demonstration carry the weight |
| 5     | Identity             | The market believes nothing and buys who it becomes                             |

Reading the level from research: everyone shouting the same outcome with no how means level 2 or 3, and a named mechanism is the opening. Everyone naming mechanisms means level 4, and the play is stronger proof or an easier version. Winners barely mentioning the product means level 5; lead with identity. Never escalate further than the market requires. A mechanism must be real.

**One promo, one stage.** A single post that walks a cold reader all the way to an offer fails at both ends. Across a month the mix covers several stages; each piece owns its own.

## 2. The six leads

The lead is the opening move and the frame everything hangs on. From most direct (hot traffic) to most indirect (cold, skeptical traffic):

1. **Offer lead.** The deal itself. For most-aware and returning readers.
2. **Promise lead.** The core outcome, as concrete as sophistication allows. For product- and solution-aware readers in markets below level 3.
3. **Problem lead.** Name the pain more precisely than they can, agitate, turn to the solution. The workhorse for problem-aware readers.
4. **Mechanism lead** (the secret). The intriguing how: a named method or a counterintuitive cause. For solution-aware readers in sophisticated markets. Must pay off the intrigue.
5. **Proclamation lead.** A bold, verifiable statement that stops the scroll. For low-awareness readers. Must be substantiated.
6. **Story lead.** A narrative the reader wants to finish: a founder or customer transformation. For unaware and skeptical readers and level 5 markets.

Choosing: awareness sets how direct the lead may be; sophistication sets whether the substance is claim, mechanism, proof, or identity. When two leads fit, prefer the more direct one. Never stack leads in one piece.

## 3. Hooks and the four tests

The first line has one job: make the diagnosed reader stop and grant the next sentence. What earns the stop: self-interest stated concretely (a number, a timeframe, a named situation), genuine news, curiosity the piece pays off, a call-out that names the reader (relevance at the cost of reach), a pattern interrupt used once.

Write variants that differ by **angle**, never by synonym: one problem call-out, one mechanism tease, one proof-forward line, one identity or story open, one offer-direct line when awareness permits.

Before shipping, every hook passes all four tests. Kill any that fails one.

1. Would the diagnosed reader recognize themselves in the first line?
2. Is there one concrete detail a competitor could not paste into their own copy?
3. Does the piece pay off what the hook opens?
4. Could the claim survive being asked for proof?

## 4. The claim ladder and the proof hierarchy

Body copy climbs in order, and each rung earns the next:

1. **Promise**, sized to the market.
2. **Mechanism**, the named real reason.
3. **Proof**, why a stranger should believe it.
4. **Offer**, what they get and why now.

In sophisticated markets the mechanism rung carries the most weight. For most-aware readers the ladder collapses toward the offer.

Proof, strongest to weakest: demonstration; specific numbers and receipts; testimonials from people like the reader answering a real objection; aggregate numbers; borrowed authority; bare assertion. Use the strongest proof the person truly has, place it next to the claim it supports, and say what must be shown rather than said. Never fabricate testimonials, statistics, results, or reviews. No income or health promises the offer cannot substantiate. Flag every claim the person must be able to prove.

## 5. Writing rules

- One reader, singular, in the voice of a smart friend who knows this problem cold. Copy that addresses a crowd stops converting.
- Enter the conversation already running in the reader's head.
- Specificity is the style. Exact numbers beat rounded claims. Facts beat adjectives. Cut amazing, revolutionary, and game-changing on sight and replace each with the fact that would have earned it.
- Vary sentence length. Short paragraphs; one-line paragraphs are allowed.
- Open loops get paid off a few lines later. Transitions pull; summaries give the reader a place to stop.
- The flaw and the confession convert because they are true: "this is not for everyone, here is who should not buy."
- Price arrives after value, framed against the outcome or the cost of the alternative, anchored once, stated plainly, never apologized for.
- Guarantee stated in the reader's terms, conditions in plain sight.
- No em dashes.

## 6. The six social promotion formats

| Format                     | Shape                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Reply plug                 | One or two lines under the person's own best post of the day, in the thread's register, linking the newsletter or offer. Continues the post's idea; never changes the subject. |
| Inside-the-newsletter plug | Two to five lines placed where the piece's idea meets the offer, framed as the next step for the reader who wants the result just described.                                   |
| Standalone promo post      | A complete short piece with its own lead, one rung of proof, and a soft or hard ask matched to the stage.                                                                      |
| Story promo                | A specific moment (a customer's result, the person's own failure the offer fixes) in three to six lines, offer as the natural ending.                                          |
| Objection post             | Name the reason someone did not buy and answer it honestly.                                                                                                                    |
| Proof post                 | One receipt, shown, with one line of context.                                                                                                                                  |

Match the format to the routine: one promotion a day, most days a reply plug or inside-the-newsletter plug, standalone promos a few times a week.

## 7. Email sequences

**Launch sequence** (five to seven emails across a real window with a real close date):

1. The open: story or proclamation lead, the reframe, the offer named once.
2. The mechanism: how it works and why other things failed.
3. The proof: results and receipts, one objection answered.
4. The fit: who it is for, who it is not for, the guarantee.
5. The stakes: the cost of not deciding, honest urgency.
6. The close: the deal, the deadline, one action.
7. Optional last call on the final day.

**Evergreen sequence**: the same arc without the deadline, spaced over one to two weeks after someone joins the list. The offer is introduced softly by email two and directly by email four.

Rules for every email: one idea, one link, one call to action. Subject lines come from the headline families: outcome, mistake, reframe, question, constraint, the lowercase confessional, the "you're not [bad thing]" relief. The subject line gets the open; the first line keeps them reading; they must be different sentences. Two subject options per email, from different families.

## 8. The promotion calendar and log

- Promote once a day. Never run the same angle twice in one week.
- Rotate at least five angles across a week: problem, mechanism, proof, story or identity, offer.
- Match the day's angle to that day's content: a proof plug under a results post, a mechanism plug under a how-to post, a story plug under a personal post.
- Keep a running log in the kit: date, format, angle, lead type, where it ran, response.
- Week two reads week one's log, drops what got no response, doubles what did.
- A new launch resets the rotation and starts from the open.

## 9. Congruence

- The promo's hook is the landing page headline's starting point. A reader who clicked one promise and lands on a different one bounces.
- One page, one outcome, one call to action.
- Every promotion carries a congruence line: what the page must say above the fold for this promo to work.
- Voice congruence: promotions sound like the person at their most direct, drawn from their own posts, never like a template with their name on it.
- Urgency and scarcity are literally true or cut.

## 10. The landing page brief

Not the page. The brief hands a page writer everything the promotions already decided:

1. Awareness stage and sophistication level.
2. The chosen lead type and the winning hook to open from.
3. The offer's big idea, mechanism, guarantee, and price framing.
4. The proof available and the proof gaps.
5. Who it is for and who it is not for.
6. The one call to action.
7. The congruence line every promo points at.
