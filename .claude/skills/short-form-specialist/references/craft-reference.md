# The craft reference

The durable principles behind every post this skill writes: how to find an angle, how to open and close, the shapes a post can take, how each platform wants it, how to research without copying, and the bar a post clears before it ships. Principles only. Nothing here is material to imitate.

## An idea is not an angle

An idea is a subject. An angle is a specific claim about that subject, for a particular reader, with a reason it matters now. A strong angle has four parts: a claim the post can defend, a tension that creates curiosity, proof or texture that earns attention, and a payoff that rewards the reader.

Build angles from the creator's owned material: a decision they made, a pattern they noticed, a mistake they can explain, a result they can back up, a process they use, a question they keep hearing, an opinion they can defend, or a connection between two ideas. Public posts reveal packaging and audience language. They never lend the creator someone else's story, authority, or voice.

## Angle families

- **Counterintuitive lesson**: a reasonable assumption fails for a specific reason.
- **Costly mistake**: a familiar behavior has a hidden cost, followed by the better move.
- **Useful observation**: a concrete pattern the audience has not named yet.
- **Before and after shift**: a changed belief, process, or constraint explains a better outcome.
- **Practical framework**: a small set of decisions makes a hard task easier.
- **Evidence-backed argument**: examples or data support a clear interpretation.
- **Personal realization**: a real event changes how the creator understands a broader problem.
- **Timely reaction**: a current event or market signal matters because of a durable principle.
- **Problem to solution**: the reader recognizes one painful situation and gets a usable next step.

Do not force contrarian language where the evidence only supports a useful clarification. Novelty without truth makes a brittle post.

## Generative moves for mining one source

Each of the five angles in a run should come from a different move:

- **Hidden mechanism**: name the process under a visible outcome. "You need discipline" is advice; explaining why announcing a goal substitutes for doing it is a mechanism.
- **Useful distinction**: separate two things people collapse (information versus progress, visibility versus memorability, attention versus trust). A distinction earns a post when it changes a decision.
- **Counterexample**: a case that weakens common advice, real or clearly hypothetical, used to improve the claim, never to manufacture controversy.
- **Personal observation**: a specific scene or realization widened into a pattern. Never invent the scene.
- **Practical sequence**: a result turned into ordered decisions where the order matters.
- **Tradeoff**: what a desirable choice costs, or what a shortcut removes.
- **Second-order effect**: what happens after the obvious result.
- **Identity shift**: the kind of operator the reader is becoming, drawn from the source, never shaming.

## The independence test

For each candidate angle write a one-line claim and a one-line payoff. If two claims differ only in wording, they are one angle. If two openings lead to the same payoff, they are one angle. Changing only the hook is not a new angle. When two angles fail the test, replace one.

Rank by truth, relevance, distinctiveness, support, and fit with the creator's goal. A quieter, better-supported angle beats a louder unsupported one.

## Pressure test before drafting

Can the post make one promise and keep it? Does the creator have the right to make the claim? Is there a concrete example, mechanism, or observation? Would a skeptical reader see why it matters now? Is it distinct from the source rather than a paraphrase? Is it small enough for short form?

## Opening families

- **Direct claim**: the central belief in the clearest useful words. Never add "unpopular opinion" to make an ordinary claim sound brave.
- **Problem recognition**: a moment the reader recognizes. "You have seventeen saved courses and no finished project" is a scene; "you procrastinate" is a label.
- **Mechanism reveal**: name why an outcome keeps happening.
- **Contrast**: two commonly confused ideas side by side, where the contrast changes a decision.
- **Story tension**: begin where something changed, failed, or surprised. Never fabricate the story, and never hold basic context hostage.
- **Proof or observation**: a verified result or repeated pattern with enough context to mean something. A big number without a denominator or timeframe is decoration.
- **Practical promise**: a useful outcome and the constraint it is delivered under, proportional to the length.
- **Question**: only when the uncertainty is real or the answer reframes something.

Opening checks: the first line tells the right reader why this matters, needs no other post for context, does not overstate the evidence, and does not force borrowed aggression into the creator's voice. Cut throat-clearing, topic labels, and announcements that the post is important.

## Ending families

- **Consequence**: what the claim changes in practice.
- **Reframe**: back to the opening problem with a more useful model that follows from the post.
- **Compressed principle**: the argument in one memorable line, never generic inspiration.
- **Decision or action**: the next useful move, specific and connected to the mechanism.
- **Image or callback**: return to a scene, phrase, or contrast from earlier.
- **Honest question**: invite the reader to weigh a real tradeoff. Never a bare "thoughts?"

Audit opening and ending together. A mechanism hook needs an implication ending. A story hook needs the change resolved. A claim hook needs the middle to earn it. Across a day and a week, rotate opening and ending families; repetition makes strong posts feel generated.

## The eleven post shapes

1. **Concise assertion**: one or two sentences carry the whole claim and implication. Nowhere to hide vague reasoning.
2. **Conditional contrast**: a repeated "if X, then Y" that maps situations to responses and builds to a point.
3. **Multi-line progression**: each beat on its own line (problem, cause, reframe, implication). Never split one sentence into fragments for vertical space.
4. **Problem, mechanism, consequence**: the strongest teaching shape, because the insight earns the advice.
5. **Observation to principle**: a concrete behavior widened into a pattern, then the principle.
6. **Hook, list, payoff**: a result or tension, three to seven parallel bullets, then the deeper implication. Not for sequential ideas.
7. **Ordered sequence**: steps whose order matters and each step changes the state.
8. **Before and after**: an old model against a new one, with the cause of the shift.
9. **Myth and correction**: a common belief stated fairly, where it fails, the more precise model. No straw men.
10. **Small story**: a brief scene or failure and the lesson it earned. Only details that create stakes.
11. **Evidence-led**: a verified number or pattern, the context to read it, the implication. Label uncertainty.

Choose the shape after the claim is clear. In any batch, no single shape dominates unless the source or the creator demands it. Variety comes from different editorial moves, never from different formatting of the same move.

## Platform-native rules

- **X post**: lead with the claim or tension at once. One idea. Hook, mechanism or proof, implication, clean last line. Under 280 characters. No hashtags by default. Several independent steps means it is a thread.
- **X thread**: the first post is a complete promise. Every segment has one job. Concrete examples and transitions so it is not a list of tips. End with the synthesized takeaway. Save segments separately, in order.
- **Threads**: same instincts as an X post with a little more room and a warmer register. One idea, conversational, no hashtags by default.
- **LinkedIn**: open before the fold with a specific observation, outcome, or tension. Develop through credible experience, mechanism, or framework. Readable paragraphs; save the one-line dramatic beat for where it counts. End with a useful conclusion or a question that follows from the argument.
- **Substack Note**: written for an ongoing relationship with readers. More reflection and texture than an X post. Observation, why it matters, a concrete example or connection, an invitation to think or reply. Not a miniature newsletter intro.
- **Instagram caption**: the first line creates context before truncation. Complements the image or carousel rather than narrating it. Clear progression, relevant final action.
- **Reel, Short, or TikTok script**: written for speech and timing. A spoken promise or tension, why to stay, one argument in concrete beats, short sentences that sound natural aloud, the payoff before any call to action. A script has no media, so it is saved as a note, never as a scheduler draft.

Use a single post for one compressed insight, a thread for a multi-step argument, LinkedIn for professional proof, a Note for relational reflection, Instagram for visual-first teaching, video for ideas strengthened by delivery. Honor the platform the creator named.

## Batch variety

A batch reads like separate publishing decisions. One idea chopped into pieces is a failed batch. For ten posts from one piece, a starting balance is four concise posts, three multi-line posts, and three structured or list posts; the source decides the final mix. Each post stands alone: no setup that depends on another post. Then check the batch as a system: vary angle, opening move, emotional register, shape, and ending. If two posts leave the same takeaway, merge or replace one. The same check applies to a day's two or three posts.

## Research and originality

The creator's own workspace is the primary source of substance. Live market material is evidence about attention and structure. Keep observation and inference separate: "these five posts opened with a quantified mistake" is an observation; "the specificity likely earned attention" is analysis.

Market research: prefer posts that beat their own creator's baseline over raw popularity, because account size distorts counts. Study several relevant examples before calling something a pattern. Read the actual posts; titles and metrics alone never make a pattern. Include counterexamples when winner-only evidence would overstate the conclusion. Cap the readout at five bullets: the pattern, the counter-signal, the open lane, and receipts only for posts actually retrieved.

**Skeleton only.** A hook's shape is fair to reuse: the sentence frame with the creator-specific parts lifted out and refilled with this creator's own claim, numbers, and story. Never fair: the source's distinctive language, stories, figures, results, voice, or an argument reproduced beat for beat. Never reuse a run of eight or more words. Transform references by changing the claim, evidence, context, and payoff, never the synonyms.

**Voice.** The creator's own posts are primary. Learn sentence density, rhythm, vocabulary, confidence, humor, directness, and preferred shapes. Do not copy memorable phrases to fake resemblance. Other people's posts are structural evidence and never authorize impersonation.

Never fabricate a quote, result, customer, metric, personal story, or source read. If proof is missing, narrow the claim, ask for the proof, or label the gap. Research is done when more of it would not change the claim, platform, or draft. Then write.

## The nine-point rubric (silent preflight, visible on request)

1. **Hook**: a specific claim, tension, result, or question, fast, with honest curiosity.
2. **One central idea**: a reader can state the claim in one sentence; every line advances it.
3. **Specificity**: concrete nouns, decisions, mechanisms, examples. "I cut the form from nine fields to three" beats "simplicity converts", when it is true.
4. **Proof and honesty**: proof strength matches claim strength. Experience, example, data, and interpretation are told apart.
5. **Progression**: each beat creates a reason to read the next. No restating the hook.
6. **Voice**: a thoughtful version of the creator; never a creator template. No fake vulnerability or borrowed mannerisms.
7. **Originality**: a claim, synthesis, application, or disagreement the creator can own.
8. **Platform fit**: length, pacing, line breaks, evidence density, and ending match the destination.
9. **Ending**: completes the promise, sharpens the implication, or invites a relevant action. Never a summary, never disconnected engagement bait.

Revision order: premise and proof, then focus and progression, then platform fit, then sentences. When fixing a draft, present at most the three changes that matter most, then the improved post. A post is ready when it is honest, specific, coherent, native, and useful enough to publish without an apology.

## Scheduler handoff rules

Drafting never grants permission to publish. Saving to the Scheduler as a draft is the safe default handoff: the creator opens it, edits, picks the time. Scheduling a time or publishing now happens only when the creator explicitly asks in that conversation; "add it to my scheduler" is not permission to schedule. Always confirm the destination account before handing anything over, send thread segments in order, and never claim success the tool did not report. On failure, keep the copy in chat, explain the error plainly, and offer to retry without creating duplicates.
