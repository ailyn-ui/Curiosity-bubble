# Offer craft reference

The durable method behind the Offer Consultant. Read the part that matches the current step: choosing a first product, the value equation audit, market and pricing judgment, stack construction, enhancers and guarantees, or the blueprint contract. Everything here is judgment applied to one person's facts. Never apply it in the abstract.

## Choosing a first product

A one-person business sells one of four things. Each has a different cost, speed, and proof requirement.

**Coaching or a service.** Done-with-you or done-for-you, sold to a few people at a time. Fastest to first dollar, highest perceived value, needs the least audience. Costs time and does not scale. Best first product when the person has skill but no audience proof yet: the first ten clients teach them what the product should be, and the call transcripts become the course later.

**A digital product.** A template, guide, toolkit, swipe file, or short paid resource. Cheap to make, low price, sells while they sleep. Needs distribution to matter, and a low price attracts customers who do not act. Best when the person already has attention and one repeatable result they can package in a weekend. Also the right lead product in front of a bigger offer.

**A course or cohort.** A structured path to one outcome, recorded or live. Higher price, scales, and the cohort version adds real urgency and accountability. Needs a proven process and enough audience to fill it. Do not build a course before the process has produced the result for other people at least a few times.

**A small software or AI tool.** An app, an automation, a custom AI, or a paid workflow that performs a repeatable task. Highest scalability, recurring revenue possible, and now buildable by one person. Needs a task that is truly repeatable, ongoing maintenance, and the person's appetite to build and support it. Best when the outcome is a job to be done rather than a skill to be learned, and when the person enjoys building.

**Decision rules:**

1. Start where demand already showed up: what people already ask them for, which posts already get saved and replied to.
2. Pick the smallest thing that still delivers the whole outcome.
3. No audience proof yet: sell time first, productize later.
4. Outcome is a repeatable task: consider the tool. Outcome is a skill: consider the course.
5. Match the format to the audience's purchasing power and to how much of the person's week they are willing to sell.
6. One first product. A second product is a second offer.

Present the four options as a compact comparison (fit with skill, fit with the audience's purchasing power, time to first sale, main risk), then recommend one in two lines.

## The value equation

Perceived value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (time delay × effort and sacrifice).

The two top levers raise what the prospect believes they get. The two bottom levers lower what it costs them in time and pain. Price is not in the equation: an offer that maximizes it supports a premium price, one that ignores it has to discount.

- **Dream outcome**: the state the prospect wants, never the product's function. Nobody wants a course; they want the income, the audience, the body. State it in their words and size it honestly to what this product can produce.
- **Perceived likelihood**: the belief that it will work for them. Raised by proof from people like them, a named mechanism that explains why this works when other things failed, specificity, and risk reversal. Most weak offers die here: a big promise with no reason to believe subtracts value.
- **Time delay**: how long until the first meaningful result. Shrink it structurally, never rhetorically: a fast win in week one, a done-for-you head start, a template that produces something on day one. Then name the honest timeframe.
- **Effort and sacrifice**: what the prospect must give up or push through. Reduce it by changing the delivery mechanism (done-with-you beats do-it-yourself), removing steps, or absorbing the hard parts. Reframe honestly what cannot be removed; hidden effort produces refunds.

**The audit:** walk the four levers in order and score each. Rebuild the weakest lever first; improving a strong one is polish. Never inflate a lever with a claim the person cannot substantiate. Shrinking time delay is often the cheapest way to raise perceived likelihood. The blueprint states which levers the offer pulls hardest and which stay weak.

## Market judgment

Judge the market on four traits before constructing anything:

- **Pain**: the audience must feel the problem enough to pay to end it. A painkiller market supports stronger offers than a vitamin market. An unaware audience must first be shown the pain.
- **Purchasing power**: a perfect offer to people who cannot pay is a hobby. If the audience is broke, recommend a cheaper delivery mechanism, a different segment, or a different market.
- **Ease of targeting**: they must gather somewhere, search for something, or follow someone.
- **Growth**: a growing market forgives imperfect offers; a shrinking one punishes good ones.

**Niching multiplies price.** The same mechanism sold to everyone earns commodity pricing. Narrowed to a specific person with a specific problem in a specific situation, it earns a premium, because "this was built for people exactly like me" raises perceived likelihood. When an offer feels weak, narrowing the who is usually cheaper than strengthening the what.

Market judgments made from an interview are inferences: mark them and say what evidence would confirm them. Recommend switching markets only when pain or purchasing power is genuinely absent.

## Awareness levels

Where the audience starts decides how the offer must be framed:

1. **Unaware**: does not know they have the problem.
2. **Problem-aware**: feels it, does not know solutions exist.
3. **Solution-aware**: knows the category, not this product.
4. **Product-aware**: knows this product, not convinced.
5. **Most-aware**: ready, wants the deal.

Every blueprint component must fit the diagnosed level. A problem-aware audience needs the Big Problem and pain points to do the work; a most-aware audience needs the deal, the guarantee, and the price.

## Pricing posture

Price communicates. A premium price raises perceived value and attracts customers who act. A low price attracts customers who do not act and starves delivery quality.

Price on value delivered: a fraction of the quantified outcome. Never cost-plus, never competitor-matching. The gap between price and perceived value is the offer; widen it by raising perceived value, never by racing price down.

If the person resists premium pricing, show the arithmetic: the outcome, what capturing a fraction of it is worth, and what the current price says about the promise. When the number for the outcome is missing, say the pricing is provisional and name the proof gap.

## Constructing the stack

1. **List every problem** the prospect hits on the way to the dream outcome: before buying, during, and after. Include the ones the product does not address yet ("no time", "tried before and failed", "won't know if it's working", "my situation is different").
2. **Turn each problem into a solution** as an outcome statement, "how to get X without Y", ignoring cost for now.
3. **Choose a delivery vehicle** for each, graded on value to the prospect against cost to the seller. The craft is high value at low cost: a template that saves ten hours, a checklist that removes a failure mode, a small group critique that feels personal. The biggest value jump is often a format change rather than more content.
4. **Trim** what is low value regardless of cost and what is high cost without proportionate value. What survives is the stack. Each item earns its place by killing a named objection or shortening a lever. Padding erodes believability.

**Name the mechanism:** a descriptive, ownable label for the way this offer produces the outcome, laid out in three to six real phases of delivery. A renamed table of contents is not a mechanism. Features in the blueprint always pair with the obstacle they remove. The stack serves one dream outcome; a second outcome is a second offer.

## Enhancers and guarantees

Every enhancer has an honest version and a manipulative version. Only the honest one is allowed, because a caught lie destroys perceived likelihood.

- **Scarcity**: real capacity limits only, with the reason stated. No fake counters.
- **Urgency**: real deadlines only, such as a cohort start or a true price change. If there is no natural deadline, create a real one rather than faking one.
- **Bonuses**: stack items framed as additions, each killing a named objection or shortening time to result. Three sharp bonuses beat ten fillers.

Guarantees move risk from buyer to seller. Creativity matters more than size.

| Type           | What it is                                                                                  | Right for                                                                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Unconditional  | Money back, no questions                                                                    | Low-touch products; the safe default                                           |
| Conditional    | Do the work; no result, refund. Conditions achievable and checkable                         | High-touch offers                                                              |
| Outcome-based  | Tied to the quantified outcome ("book three clients in sixty days or we keep working free") | The strongest form, only when the seller can honestly back it                  |
| Anti-guarantee | All sales final, because X                                                                  | Instant-consumption products where the rest of the offer carries believability |

Design the guarantee against the actual feared risk (wasting money, wasting time, looking foolish). Ask about refund tolerance before recommending an aggressive one. Every enhancer must survive "is this literally true?"

## The Offer Blueprint note

One editable note per offer, titled `Offer Blueprint: [product]`, with exactly these sections in order:

1. Target Audience (one tight paragraph ending with the awareness level)
2. Delivery Mechanism (the format and why)
3. Big Idea (one concise articulation of the promise)
4. Big Problem (the primary pain, framed for the awareness level)
5. Irking Pain Points (a progressive list, each sharper than the last)
6. Quantifiable Outcome (a measurable result with a timeframe)
7. Risk Reversal (the guarantee with conditions stated plainly)
8. Unique Mechanism (the named system in three to six steps)
9. Features and Benefits (each line pairs a feature with the obstacle it removes)
10. Value Equation Analysis (four short entries: what the offer does for each lever and where it is weak)
11. Positioning Recommendations (how to communicate the offer across the person's channels)
12. Pricing (the recommended price, the arithmetic behind it, any lead product in front of it)

When phase one ran, a short First Product Decision section sits at the top. Mark every component built by inference rather than verified fact as a suggestion. Revise the same note; never create a second blueprint for the same product. Keep the interview, commentary, and next steps out of the note.
