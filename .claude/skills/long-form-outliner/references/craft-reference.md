# Craft reference

The durable principles behind the outliner. Read the relevant part when you choose a structure route, build a title or subject line, map re-hooks, or set budgets. These are principles, never scripts to imitate.

## One promise from package to payoff

A newsletter or a video is one promise system. The title (or subject line) creates an expectation. The first line (or first five seconds) confirms it. The opening sharpens it into a useful promise. The body earns it through progression and proof. The ending resolves it.

Treat these as one thing. A strong title cannot rescue an opening that changes the subject. A clever hook cannot rescue a body that stops progressing. A useful body still disappoints when the payoff leaves the opening question unanswered.

Write the opening contract as one sentence before building anything:

> This piece helps [reader] move from [starting state] to [promised change] through [mechanism], supported by [proof].

Compare it to the title, the opening, the beats, and the payoff. If they describe different pieces, repair the weakest part. Do not make every part more dramatic.

## Confirm the click before adding context

The reader should recognize the piece they chose in the first line. Confirmation can be a concise line, visible evidence, the promised object or result, or a direct continuation of the title's tension. Only after confirmation does the opening add the consequence, the specific outcome, the curiosity gap, the reason to trust this writer, and the path ahead. Context that delays confirmation creates doubt. Never open with "In this newsletter" or "Today I want to talk about".

## Every beat has a distinct job

A strong section changes the reader's understanding: it reveals a cause, replaces a model, proves a mechanism, answers an objection, shows an application, or raises the consequence. For each beat know five things: the reader's question, the new claim, the evidence, why it follows the previous beat, and the next unanswered question it creates. Two beats doing the same job get combined. A beat that changes nothing gets cut or rebuilt.

## Curiosity needs planned closure

An open loop is an unanswered question the reader understands and cares about. Resolve it at a deliberate point. The best re-hook follows a partial payoff: the first solution reveals a second problem, an example challenges the model, a result raises the question of why it worked, a useful principle creates a harder application. Track every loop through the outline so nothing stays open by accident. No arbitrary teasers.

## Credibility

Credibility answers "why should I trust this version of the answer?" It comes from direct experience, visible work, transparent research, a demonstration, a case, or sourced evidence. Use the lightest proof that supports the claim. Match certainty to evidence: a case study supports "what happened", never "the universal rule". A narrower honest claim beats a louder unsupported one.

## Endings

Resolve the opening promise before asking for anything. Never close with "thanks for reading" or a generic call to action. End with one of three moves: an open question you do not fully answer, a resonant image that stays with the reader, or an identity reframe (the reader sees that the real question was a different one all along). A next-step ask may open a new question, but it must never withhold the answer this piece promised. The primary call to action comes after the resolution.

## The six structure routes

Choose the route that best transforms the reader using material the writer can actually support. Routes differ in logic and progression, never in title. Present three per session, as route cards.

**Contrarian argument.** Use when the writer holds a defensible view that challenges common advice. Progression: make the accepted belief recognizable; show its cost or contradiction; explain why it persists; introduce the better model; prove it; answer the strongest objection; convert the reframe into a decision. Risk: manufactured disagreement. Represent the opposing belief fairly and bring evidence beyond confidence.

**Story-led transformation.** Use when a real experience, project, or failure carries the proof. Progression: open at a consequential moment or visible result; establish the earlier state and belief; introduce the obstacle or failed approach; reveal the turning point; test the new model through concrete events; extract the principle without flattening the story; translate it into the reader's next step. Risk: chronology without consequence. Every scene must change the reader's understanding.

**Framework or tutorial.** Use when the reader wants to make, decide, diagnose, or improve something. Progression: show the desired result and why common attempts fail; give a map; establish the first principle; move through dependent steps with proof at each; surface the failure mode most likely to break it; show the completed system in context; give the smallest useful first action. Risk: a flat listicle. Later steps must depend on earlier understanding.

**Research-led explainer.** Use when the question is contested, changing, or counterintuitive. Progression: frame the consequential question; show why the obvious answer is incomplete; define scope and evidence lanes; present the strongest pattern; test it against counterevidence; synthesize what is supported, uncertain, and likely; say what the reader should now believe or do. Risk: information without a decision.

**Case study.** Use when one person, company, project, or event makes a mechanism visible. Progression: show the result or anomaly; establish the starting constraints; reconstruct the important choices; separate cause from correlation and hindsight; identify what generalizes; state what does not; apply it to the reader. Risk: survivorship bias and unsupported causality.

**The list.** Use when the writer has five or more distinct points and wants authority and value density. Open with why the list matters to this reader personally. Each item is one idea plus one sentence of elaboration; tease depth rather than over-explaining. Risk: a pile of tips with no throughline. The list still needs a reframe holding it together.

Score routes on: fit with the reader and the promised transformation, strength of proof, distinctiveness in the niche, fit with the writer's real view and voice, ability to sustain the length, opening and payoff potential. A hybrid is valid only when one route stays the dominant spine.

## Title and subject-line families

A title family describes the expectation the headline creates. Vary the underlying promise across options, never just the wording.

- **Outcome**: names a desirable change, may hint at the mechanism. "Build a useful reading system without saving more articles."
- **Mistake**: a costly behavior and an implied correction. "Your weekly plan is creating more unfinished work."
- **Reframe**: challenges the reader's current model. "More ideas will never fix a weak point of view."
- **Experiment**: a bounded attempt and its real result. "I published one useful note a day for 30 days." Never manufacture a timeframe or an outcome.
- **Case study**: the lesson through a real person, company, or event.
- **Question**: a genuine uncertainty the reader wants resolved. "Is your niche too small, or is your promise too vague?"
- **System**: an organized, repeatable method. "The three-pass system I use to turn research into scripts."
- **Comparison**: helps choose between credible alternatives. No false binaries.
- **Transformation**: a real starting and ending state. "From scattered notes to a weekly body of work."
- **Constraint**: specificity through a meaningful limit. "Grow a research habit with 20 minutes a week." The constraint must shape the method.
- **Identity and stakes**: who the reader is becoming, or the cost of ignoring the problem. Powerful and easy to abuse. Ground it in the argument; no shame, no existential exaggeration.

Subject-line shapes that work especially well for newsletters: "You're not [bad thing]: [good thing]" (relieves shame, promises a fix); "Give me [time] and I'll [transformation]"; "If [condition], [directive]" (self-selects the reader); the lowercase confessional (reads like a text from a friend); "How to [surprising pairing]". The subject line gets the open; the opening line keeps the reader. They must be different sentences.

## Skeleton, never substance

Every winning headline has two layers with opposite rules. The **skeleton** is the frame with the creator-specific parts lifted out: "After [studying N of a thing], here's how to [specific outcome]", "Stop [common default]. Do this instead." Reuse the skeleton; that is shared craft. The **substance** is the source creator's own numbers, names, story, product, and result. It never travels.

Write the skeleton in brackets before filling it with the writer's own evidence. Test: if a proposed title would survive being moved to a different piece unchanged, the substance was never filled in. It is unfinished.

## Selecting a package

Judge each option on: relevance (does the right reader self-select?), clarity (can they say what value the piece holds?), curiosity (a meaningful gap they understand), specificity (concrete and supported), credibility (the writer has the proof the title implies), originality (judged on substance, never on structure), saturation (a common promise raises the bar for distinction), and promise integrity (title, opening, body, and payoff all fulfill the same expectation).

Promise integrity is the gate. Recommend one primary option and two credible alternatives that test different expectations. Never rank fifteen options with invented scores.

## Budgets

**Newsletter, 1,200 to 2,000 words:** opening claim 75 to 150; credibility 150 to 250; promise and map 50 to 100; diagnosis 250 to 400; framework parts 2 to 5, each 150 to 300; application 300 to 500; synthesis 100 to 200; primary call to action 40 to 100.

**Script:** express the same beats as a share of runtime. Roughly: hook and opening 8 to 12 percent; credibility and map 5 to 8 percent; diagnosis 12 to 18 percent; the body beats 45 to 55 percent; application 10 to 15 percent; payoff and close 5 to 8 percent. Teaching pace is about 140 to 160 spoken words a minute; use it to translate a word budget into minutes and back.

## Emotional arc

Start where the reader is (comfortable, recognized). Make the problem real (slightly uncomfortable). Resolve with hope: there is a way out and it is simpler than they think. Name the register at each beat so the outline carries the arc as well as the information.
