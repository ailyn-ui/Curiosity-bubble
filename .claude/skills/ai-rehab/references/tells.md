# AI tell catalog

Each entry says what the tell looks like and how to fix it warm. The script finds many of these, and the rest need a reader. Treat each tell as a candidate: if the writer's voice or a protected quote owns it, keep it (see "Order of priority" in SKILL.md).

## 1. Dashes

Em dashes, double hyphens used as dashes, and spaced en dashes.

- Fix with "and", a comma, or a period, whichever keeps the sentence flowing. Do not chop the line into fragments.
- "The new menu is live — come try it." becomes "The new menu is live, so come try it."

## 2. "Not X, it's Y" pivots

A straw man knocked down to sound wise. It hides in many shapes: "It's not X. It's Y.", "This isn't about X. It's about Y.", "You don't need X. You need Y.", "Not because X, but because Y.", "not just X but Y", "They don't. They need Y.", "rather than merely", "not simply", "is not just", "less X, more Y".

- Keep the contrast when it is the piece's main point, the thing the writer is pushing back on. A reader would say the piece argues against X. Say it once, in one plain sentence: "It's not about working longer. It's about working on the right things." becomes "Working on the right things beats working longer."
- Keep the "not X" half when it carries a fact, a reassurance, a limit, or a promise the reader would miss ("No contract, and you can cancel any month"). A reason the writer rules out is a fact: "Not because sales dropped" tells the reader that sales held up.
- Otherwise the "not X" half is a runway: delete it and check that the line still tells the reader everything they need. "It's not just a planner. It's a habit." becomes "It's a habit."
- Never turn a stance into a new fact about what someone did or decided.
- "It's not just an app — it's a game-changer." is cut when the next sentence already says what the app does. If nothing else says it, keep the claim plain: "The app made a big difference."

## 3. Three-beat rhythm and staccato

Three parallel beats, one-word sentences, and slogan rhythm: "Plan. Post. Profit.", "Fewer emails. Calmer mornings. Better sleep.", "No hassle. No stress. Just results."

- Fix: one concrete sentence that says what happens. A real list keeps however many items it has, since the drumbeat is the problem.
- "Fewer emails. Calmer mornings. Better sleep." becomes "You get fewer emails, calmer mornings, and better sleep." Build the sentence from the draft's own words and add nothing, not even a link between the items ("fewer emails mean calmer mornings") the draft never made.

## 4. Throat-clearing, announced importance, and announcement voice

Lines that stall, or tell the reader something matters instead of showing it: "Here's the thing", "The truth is", "At the end of the day", "In today's fast-paced world", "Let that sink in", "Here's what nobody tells you", "It's worth noting", "This changes everything", "matters because", "without requiring".

- Label openers belong here: "Important update:", "Quick note:", "PSA:".
- So does office and template phrasing: "please find attached", "at your earliest convenience", "rest assured", "kindly note that". Say it the way the writer would say it out loud.
- Announcement voice is the press-release frame around news: "We're excited to share", "Big news!", "We're over the moon". Lead with the news itself, and keep any real feeling the writer states about it.
- Fix: delete the frame and start with the point. "Here's the thing — most clients never read the fine print." becomes "Most clients never read the fine print."

## 5. Buzzwords

Words that sound busy and say nothing: "leverage", "unlock", "elevate", "empower", "streamline", "seamless", "robust", "holistic", "game-changer", "next-level", "cutting-edge", "transformative", "journey", "deep dive", "lean into".

- Fix: name what happens, for whom. "Unlock your team's true potential" becomes the actual result the draft gives, such as "get back the 18 hours a week".
- Hype with no claim behind it loses the hype and keeps the claim: "a game-changer for new bakers" becomes "made for new bakers". A big claim stays at full strength, said plainly: "This revolutionary mixer will transform baking forever" becomes "This mixer will change baking forever."

## 6. AI vocabulary

Words AI reaches for to sound weighty: "delve", "tapestry", "testament", "multifaceted", "nuanced", "resonate", "paramount", "pivotal", "meticulous", "furthermore", "moreover", "in conclusion", "underscores", "showcases", "boasts", "serves as", "stands as", "plays a vital role", "crucial", "enhance", "bolstered", "interplay", "vibrant", "nestled", "ever-evolving".

- Fix: use the plain verb ("is", "has", "shows") or cut the word. "boasts more than 20 years in roofing" becomes "has more than 20 years in roofing".

## 7. Filler words

"genuinely", "honestly", "actually", "really", "truly", "simply", "seamlessly", "quietly", "deeply", "literally", "basically".

- Fix: delete it. Keep it only when it carries a fact ("She closed the door quietly").

## 8. Colon labels

A label, a colon, then the point, like a slide: "The bottom line:", "The key insight:", "The result:", "My take:".

- Fix: drop the label and say the point. Labels that organize real facts, like "Regular price:" or "Dates:", are not tells.
- "The result: no-shows fell by half." becomes "That cut our no-shows in half." Keep the link to the cause, and add nothing.

## 9. "-ing" verdict tails

A comma and an "-ing" phrase that grades what came before: ", highlighting the importance of", ", underscoring our commitment", ", reflecting broader trends".

- Fix: cut the tail, or turn it into a sentence with a fact.

## 10. Fake-deep lines and things given feelings

Lines you could print on a candle ("room to grow", "journey of self-discovery", "and for the first time, I could breathe") and objects with feelings ("a platform that listens", "data that tells a story").

- Fix: say what happened, using the draft's own details. "I finally found my rhythm" becomes "I started blocking Monday mornings for planning" when the draft says so. If it doesn't, keep the meaning plain: "I got into a routine."

## 11. Engagement bait

"Sound familiar?", "What if you could…?", "Tag someone who needs this", "Like if you agree", "Drop a comment".

- Fix: make a statement. A real question that makes the reader think about their own work can stay, once.

## 12. Chatbot leftovers, placeholders, and hidden characters

Text copied out of a chatbot: "I hope this helps!", "Certainly! Here's", "Great question", "Feel free to reach out", "As an AI", citation junk like "utm_source=chatgpt.com", "oaicite", "[cite: 1]", and hidden characters (zero-width spaces, narrow no-break spaces).

- Fix: remove the junk and hidden characters. Hidden characters are counted, never treated as proof of anything.
- A canned sign-off becomes the same offer in plainer words, built from what the draft already says, with no new channel, time, person, or promise. "Feel free to reach out with any questions" can become "If you have any questions, ask me." It cannot become an offer to call, reply by a date, or book a time the draft never mentioned.
- A merge field the writer's email tool fills ("[First Name]", "{{first_name}}") is protected text and stays as written.
- A placeholder that asks the writer to fill something in ("[Insert name]", "[your link here]") stays where it is, because only the writer has the answer. Count it as kept on purpose and list it under "Only you can add or fix".

## 13. Endings

- A summary bow: a tidy one-liner that wraps the piece ("In conclusion, small steps add up."). Fix: stop when the point is made. If the bow holds the piece's main claim and no other line states it, keep the claim once, in plain words, inside the paragraph where it fits. A bow that adds a new word to the claim ("a necessity" after "a strategy") states something new, so keep it. When unsure, keep it, folded into its paragraph. If the bow is itself a "not X, it's Y" line, section 2 decides whether its "not X" half stays.
- Punch-punch-punch commands ("Book the call. Raise the price. Win.") and slogan closers ("Your future self will thank you!"). Fix: end warm and plain, with the real next step.
- A recap list of what the reader can now do. Fix: cut it.

## 14. Openers

Stacked one-line paragraphs at the top, each a little drumroll. Fix: open with one connected thought that says the point.

Wind-up openers ("I'm reaching out to", "I just wanted to say", "I thought I'd share") delay the point. Fix: start with what the writer wants to say. Keep a wind-up the writer clearly uses as their own way of being polite. A line that says who said a quote ("Here's what one client told us.") carries who said it, so keep that, in fewer words if you like.

## 15. Vague claims and stock praise

"recently", "a lot of", "significant results", "many clients", "more than ever". Fix: use the draft's real number or name. If the draft has none, keep the sentence and list the missing detail on the card under "Only you can add or fix". Never invent the number, and never leave a placeholder in the text.

Stock ad praise ("you deserve", "treat yourself", "indulge in", "the ultimate") sells a feeling instead of the offer. Fix: say what the offer is, with the draft's own details. A word that describes the offer ("hand-poured", "locally roasted", "small-batch") is a claim, so it stays.

## 16. Inflated credentials

Bios and About pages: "visionary leader", "seasoned entrepreneur", "featured in", "renowned", "industry-leading". Fix: keep the facts (years, clients, places) and drop the praise.

## 17. Formatting tells

- Emoji bullets: a list whose lines each start with an emoji, or emojis used as section markers. Fix: plain bullets or sentences, and if a marker emoji has a matching partner around the same heading, both go. One emoji at the start of a single line is decoration, not a bullet, so it stays, along with every other emoji, unless the user says "drop emojis".
- Bold on the start of every bullet. Fix: bold only what a reader would otherwise miss.
- Title Case On Every Heading Word. Fix: sentence case. Names inside the heading keep their capitals. The compare lists heading words whose capitals you changed as recased, which is fine.
- Hashtags stay as the writer wrote them (see `channels.md`).

## 18. Over-correction traps

Fixes that create new tells:

- Cutting dashes and triads until every sentence is short and choppy. Join thoughts with "and" and commas instead.
- Swapping a banned word for a fancier synonym ("leverage" to "harness"). Use the plain word.
- Cycling synonyms to avoid repeating a word. Repeat the plain word, or rewrite the sentence so it needs the word once.
- Making a warm line cold, or a polite ask blunt.
- Sanding off mixed feelings or odd, specific details. Keep them. They are the most human part.

## 19. House style

These come from The Uncommon Business's Anti-AI Writing Rules. They apply unless the writer's voice owns the habit:

- No semicolons. Use a period or a comma.
- No trailing ellipses. An ellipsis may join two connected ideas.
- No rhetorical questions, except a real "mirror" question that makes the reader examine their own work.
- Swearing stops at "damn".
- Tighten as you go: cut words that carry no claim, and keep every fact and step.
